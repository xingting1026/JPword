# Presentation Modality, Interaction Format and Learning Environment for L2 Vocabulary Learning

Scope: evidence on (a) background music / ambient sound, (b) presentation format (isolated pairs vs sentences vs pictures vs audio), (c) interaction types (typed recall, MC, self-graded flashcards, cloze, speaking aloud), (d) gamification/motivation, (e) session structure and sleep. Each subtopic ends with a one-line design implication for a self-study Japanese vocabulary web app used by a Taiwanese (Mandarin L1) learner.

Evidence-grade legend used below: **[META]** = meta-analysis; **[REP]** = replicated / multi-study; **[SINGLE]** = single study; **[CONTESTED]** = results conflict across studies.

---

## Key Question 1: Background music and ambient sound — help or hurt? On/off by default? Does lyrical music specifically hurt? Does target-language music help?

### Takeaway
The best-replicated finding is that *speech-like* sound (intelligible lyrics, background speech) reliably impairs verbal encoding and reading, while instrumental music has a small, unreliable effect that depends on the person (working-memory capacity, arousal) and the task. For paired-associate FL vocabulary learning specifically, de Groot's 2006 "music helps" result failed to replicate (Küssner et al. 2016), and lyrics in a language the learner understands hurt during the session. No study shows target-language background songs improving paired-associate vocabulary learning; the only positive "music" evidence is for *actively singing* the material, which is a different task.

### Cited Findings

**Theory and lab-memory literature (irrelevant sound effect / changing-state)**
- Salamé & Baddeley (1989), *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology A*, 41(1), 107–122, DOI 10.1080/14640748908402355: in serial recall of visually presented nine-digit sequences, both vocal and instrumental music disrupted short-term memory performance, with vocal music more disruptive; earlier work showed unattended speech—even in a foreign language—disrupts immediate memory for visual verbal material, while unattended noise does not, interpreted via a phonological short-term store that excludes non-speechlike sounds — [Search summary of Salamé & Baddeley 1989](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/1989-salame.pdf); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Salame+Baddeley+Effects+of+background+music+on+phonological+short-term+memory+Quarterly+Journal+of+Experimental+Psychology+1989&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Jones & Macken (1993), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 19(2), 369–381, DOI 10.1037/0278-7393.19.2.369 ("Irrelevant tones produce an irrelevant speech effect"): the irrelevant sound effect is that background speech or tones reduce serial-recall accuracy relative to noise or silence; a sequence of *different* tones impairs recall more than a single repeating tone, motivating the **changing-state hypothesis** (disruption comes from automatically processed order information in the auditory stream, O-OER model) — [PMC review of ISE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3868630); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Jones+Macken+Irrelevant+tones+produce+an+irrelevant+speech+effect+implications+for+phonological+coding+in+working+memory+1993&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)

**Meta-analyses [META]**
- Kämpfe, Sedlmeier & Renkewitz (2011), "The impact of background music on adult listeners: A meta-analysis", *Psychology of Music*, 39(4), 424–448, DOI 10.1177/0305735610376261: global analysis showed a null effect, "most probably due to averaging out specific effects"; music vs no-music comparisons indicated background music **disturbs the reading process**, has **small detrimental effects on memory**, but improves emotional reactions and sports performance; tempo of music influences tempo of concurrent activity — [Gwern-hosted PDF / abstract](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/music/music-distraction/2011-kampfe.pdf); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Kampfe+Sedlmeier+Renkewitz+impact+of+background+music+on+adult+listeners+meta-analysis&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Vasilev, Kirkby & Angele (2018), "Auditory distraction during reading: A Bayesian meta-analysis of a continuing controversy", *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 13(5), 567–597, DOI 10.1177/1745691617747398: 65 studies; background noise, speech and music all have a **small but reliably detrimental** effect on reading; **intelligible speech and lyrical music produced the biggest distraction**; disruption did not differ between adults and children; authors call for models that explain distraction by both speech and non-speech noise — [Bournemouth eprints](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/29995/)
- A secondary source reports Vasilev et al.'s overall music-on-reading estimate as Hedges's g = –0.19, and summarizes the literature as "music with lyrics is generally detrimental to cognitive performance, while instrumental music has a more minor, not credible effect" — [Journal of Cognition article citing Vasilev](https://journalofcognition.org/articles/273)

**Lyrics vs no lyrics [REP]**
- Perham & Currie (2014), "Does listening to preferred music improve reading comprehension performance?", *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, 28(2), 279–284: SAT-style reading comprehension in silence averaged 61%; non-lyrical music 55%; music with lyrics (liked or disliked) **38%** — [Learning and the Brain summary](https://learningandthebrain.com/blog/but-i-study-much-better-with-my-music-on); [Cardiff Met repository record](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/does-listening-to-preferred-music-improve-reading-comprehension-p/) (DOI not retrieved; Crossref rate-limited)

**FL vocabulary learning specifically [CONTESTED → leaning null/slightly harmful]**
- de Groot (2006), "Effects of stimulus characteristics and background music on foreign language vocabulary learning and forgetting", *Language Learning*, 56(3), 463–506, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9922.2006.00374.x: paired-associate learning of 64 Dutch–pseudoword pairs with a Bach Brandenburg Concerto No. 4 excerpt (instrumental, ~46.8 dB) vs silence; reported that music conditions resulted in more vocabulary learning than silence (in at least a subgroup of participants); also found robust stimulus effects (concrete > abstract; phonologically typical > atypical forms) — [UvA DARE record](https://www.dare.uva.nl/id/f9e7d8b3-e98c-4dd8-b5bc-6878daec925b); [FORRT replication atlas entry](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2006.00374.x/)
- Küssner, de Groot, Hofman & Hillen (2016), "EEG beta power but not background music predicts the recall scores in a foreign-vocabulary learning task", *PLOS ONE*, 11(8), e0161387, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0161387: direct replication using de Groot's identical 64 word pairs and the same Bach excerpt. Exp 1 (n=31): no music effect on immediate recall, F(1,29)=2.66, p=.114. Exp 2 (n=38): small immediate advantage for music (37.1% vs 33.2%, F(1,36)=4.61, p=.039) that **disappeared at one-week retest** (p=.088). Combined (n=69): **no effect of music**, F(1,68)=0.74, p=.392. Baseline EEG beta power (arousal) predicted recall (~10% variance). Authors ask whether "searching for an effect of background music on vocabulary learning might be a red herring" — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4990275/)
- de Groot & Smedinga (2014), "Let the music play! A short-term but no long-term detrimental effect of vocal background music with familiar language lyrics on foreign language vocabulary learning", *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 36(4), 681–707, DOI 10.1017/S0272263114000059: paired-associate learning in silence vs vocal music with **familiar-language (Dutch) lyrics** vs **unfamiliar-language (Greek) lyrics**. Learning was **poorer in the familiar-lyrics condition** than in unfamiliar-lyrics and silence during training and immediately after; the deficit was **gone at a 1-week delayed test**. Authors attribute the short-term interference to the phonological loop. Concrete words and phonologically typical forms again learned better at all timepoints — [Cambridge Core abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/let-the-music-play/C28D533232A39482804924C26DF247DF)
- Lehmann & Seufert (2017), "The influence of background music on learning in the light of different theoretical perspectives and the role of working memory capacity", *Frontiers in Psychology*, 8:1902, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01902: N=81 students learned a visual text with two fast-tempo German pop songs (medium volume, headphones) vs silence. **Recall**: no effect of music, WMC, or interaction (all F<2.37). **Comprehension**: music main effect favouring silence (F(1,73)=2.90, p=.046, η²=.04); **music × WMC interaction** (F(3,73)=3.22, p=.028, η²=.12): lowest-WMC learners scored 36.7 points higher in silence than with music (d=2.64), while higher-WMC learners were not harmed (slight linear improvement). No mediation via arousal/mood. Authors advise against background music generally because low-capacity learners are "especially impaired"; music acted as a **seductive detail** for demanding processing — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5671572/)
- A Finnish-vocabulary study on *preferred* background music exists (Jyväskylä thesis) but is not peer-reviewed; not relied upon — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/109680)

**Music *in* the target language / songs as learning material (different question from background music)**
- Ludke, Ferreira & Overy (2014), "Singing can facilitate foreign language learning", *Memory & Cognition*, 42(1), 41–52, DOI 10.3758/s13421-013-0342-5: 60 adults learned 20 English–Hungarian paired phrases by **speaking, rhythmic speaking, or singing** (listen-and-repeat); the singing group performed about twice as well as the speaking group on production/recall tests; the benefit was not explained by age, gender, mood, or musical training — [Edinburgh summary](https://culturecase-os.kdl.kcl.ac.uk/research/2014/12/singing-can-facilitate-foreign-language-learning); [PDF](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Ludke-et-al.-2014_SingingForeignLanguage_Mem-Cog.pdf). Note this is *active singing of the target phrases*, not passive background music.

### Inferences
- The mechanism is well established: anything speech-like (lyrics, podcasts, talk) competes with the phonological loop that L2 form learning depends on. Instrumental music is comparatively benign but is not reliably helpful, and the one FL-vocabulary study that found a benefit (de Groot 2006) did not survive a direct replication with the same materials.
- de Groot & Smedinga's result implies lyrics in Mandarin (or English, which a Taiwanese learner understands) are the most harmful case for this user; Japanese lyrics for a beginner would behave like "unfamiliar-language lyrics" (not harmful short-term) but as proficiency grows they would become "familiar-language lyrics" and start interfering.
- Lehmann & Seufert suggests individual differences matter: an app cannot know a user's WMC, so the safe default protects the lower-capacity learners.
- There is no evidence that passive target-language background music improves word learning; the Ludke singing effect is a production/encoding task, not an ambient-sound effect.

**Design implication for the app:** Ship with background music **off by default** (or none at all); if offered, make it opt-in, instrumental-only, low volume, and never with lyrics in Mandarin/English/Japanese; a "listen-and-repeat/sing the example sentence" feature is the only evidence-backed way to use music.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve per-condition effect sizes for lyrical vs non-lyrical music from Vasilev et al. 2018 (only the overall music estimate g ≈ –0.19 via secondary source).
- Kämpfe et al. 2011's domain-specific d-values were not legible from the retrieved PDF.
- No study located that tests passive **target-language** background songs during explicit vocabulary study; de Groot & Smedinga's "unfamiliar-language lyrics" is the closest proxy.
- Perham & Currie 2014 DOI not retrieved (Crossref rate-limited).

## Key Question 2: Word-in-isolation vs word-in-context — does an example sentence beat an isolated pair? When does context help and when is it a distraction?

### Takeaway
For *initial* form–meaning mapping, isolated L1–L2 pairs are at least as effective as sentence context and often more time-efficient; rich context can divert attention from the target form (Laufer & Shmueli; Prince), and tasks that force heavy semantic processing (e.g., sentence writing) can actively depress form learning (Barcroft's TOPRA model). Context earns its place for *depth* of knowledge (collocation, grammatical use) and for later encounters, not for the first exposure.

### Cited Findings
- Laufer & Shmueli (1997), "Memorizing new words: Does teaching have anything to do with it?", *RELC Journal*, 28(1), 89–108, DOI 10.1177/003368829702800106: context "may have the deleterious effect of moving the learner's attention away from the target word to other words or the general message of the text", so target words "may go unnoticed", especially when context is rich (sentence or paragraph); learning words isolated or in short sentence contexts was more effective than in elaborated texts, with no striking superiority of sentence context over isolated words — [CASALC review citing L&S 1997](https://journals.muni.cz/casalc-review/article/download/14192/11864); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Laufer+Shmueli+Memorizing+new+words+Does+teaching+have+anything+to+do+with+it+RELC+1997&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Prince (1996), "Second language vocabulary learning: The role of context versus translations as a function of proficiency", *Modern Language Journal*, 80(4), 478–493, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-4781.1996.tb05468.x: French learners of English learned more via **L1 translation than via L2 sentence context, especially weaker learners**; Prince did not advocate translation-only but argued weaker learners need training to exploit context and should combine both — [Equinox summary](https://api.equinoxpub.com/articles/34212); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Prince+Second+language+vocabulary+learning+the+role+of+context+versus+translations+as+a+function+of+proficiency+Modern+Language+Journal+1996&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Webb (2007), "The effects of repetition on vocabulary knowledge", *Applied Linguistics*, 28(1), 46–65, DOI 10.1093/applin/aml048: 121 Japanese university EFL students; gains in all aspects of word knowledge (orthography, meaning, grammatical functions, association, syntax) tended to increase as the number of contextual presentations increased (1, 3, 7, 10) — [Search summary](https://d.lib.msu.edu/etd/3113/OBJ/download); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Webb+The+effects+of+repetition+on+vocabulary+knowledge+Applied+Linguistics+2007&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Barcroft (2002), "Semantic and structural elaboration in L2 lexical acquisition", *Language Learning*, 52(2) (DOI not retrieved; Crossref rate-limited): semantic elaboration operationalized as **writing the new word in a sentence** produced "strong negative effects" on word-form learning compared with no-task controls, "suggesting that sentence writing can inhibit word form learning during the initial stages of L2 lexical acquisition" — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/effects-of-sentence-writing-in-second-language-lexical-acquisitio/); [EuroSLA Journal discussion](https://euroslajournal.org/articles/44)
- Barcroft's **TOPRA (Type of Processing–Resource Allocation) model**: processing resources are limited; increasing semantic processing can increase learning of semantic properties while *decreasing* learning of formal properties "because fewer processing resources remain available to process for word form"; increased semantic processing from tasks such as sentence writing "can decrease L2 word learning substantially" — [Barcroft lab page](https://sites.wustl.edu/barcroft/?p=461); [Benjamins book record](https://www.schweitzer-online.de/en/buch/Barcroft/Lexical-Input-Processing-Vocabulary-Learning/9789027213280/A32083718/)
- Boers, Warren, He & Deconinck (2017), *System*, 66, 113–129, DOI 10.1016/j.system.2017.03.017: adding pictures to glosses "may reduce the amount of attention that L2 readers give to the form of these words" — same attention-trade-off logic as TOPRA — [Search summary](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/edupub/99)
- Nakata (2015), "Effects of expanding and equal spacing on second language vocabulary learning", *SSLA*, 37(4), 677–711, DOI 10.1017/S0272263114000825: a flashcard (paired-associate) paradigm is used throughout Nakata's programme; spaced flashcards retained vocabulary at two-to-three times the rate of massed study — [Search summary](https://flashcards.world/wiki/learn-a-new-language-with-flashcards/); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Nakata+Effects+of+expanding+and+equal+spacing+on+second+language+vocabulary+learning+Does+gradually+increasing+spacing+increase+vocabulary+learning+2015&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019), "The effects of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis of correlational studies", *Language Learning*, 69(3), DOI 10.1111/lang.12343 [META]: 45 effect sizes, 26 studies, 1,918 participants; medium correlation r = .34 between frequency of encounters in context and incidental learning; moderators include spacing, visual aids, learner engagement — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/search?query=sleep+consolidation+second+language+vocabulary+learning+foreign+words&fields=title,year,venue,abstract,externalIds&limit=5)
- Webb & co-authors' incidental-learning meta-analysis (2023), *Language Teaching*, DOI 10.1017/S0261444822000507 [META]: across 24 studies / 2,771 participants, meaning-focused input yields only 9–18% of target words learned immediately and 6–17% delayed; i.e., context-only incidental learning is slow relative to deliberate study — [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/search?query=sleep+consolidation+second+language+vocabulary+learning+foreign+words&fields=title,year,venue,abstract,externalIds&limit=5)

### Inferences
- The consistent picture: deliberate pair learning is the fastest route to the form–meaning link; context adds *breadth* (collocation, grammar) rather than faster mapping, and it taxes the same limited resources needed for form encoding.
- For Japanese specifically, where form learning includes kanji + reading + pitch, the form-learning burden is unusually high, so TOPRA's warning (semantic tasks crowd out form) applies with extra force on first exposure.
- Context is most useful once the pair is established (later reviews), where it supports usage knowledge and provides an incidental repetition.

**Design implication for the app:** Present the first exposure as a clean pair (Japanese form + reading + L1 gloss) with the example sentence **collapsed/secondary**, and surface sentence-level context on later reviews or as an optional cloze step; never require sentence writing on first exposure.

### Gaps
- No direct experiment located that compares *flashcard-with-sentence* vs *flashcard-without-sentence* under time-on-task control for Japanese; the Laufer & Shmueli and Prince findings are for European-language pairs.
- Barcroft 2002 DOI and exact effect sizes not retrieved.

## Key Question 3: Pictures and multimedia — dual coding, Mayer's multimedia principle, and the overconfidence problem

### Takeaway
Pictures help when the learner must still encode and retrieve the foreign form (dual-coding/multimedia effects are real in Plass et al.), but learners are reliably overconfident with pictures and may under-study picture-cued words (Carpenter & Olson), and pictures can pull attention away from word form (Boers et al.). Pictures are safest for concrete nouns and when paired with retrieval that targets the form.

### Cited Findings
- Mayer & Sims (1994), "For whom is a picture worth a thousand words? Extensions of a dual-coding theory of multimedia learning", *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 86(3), 389–401, DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.86.3.389: concurrent (vs successive) animation + narration improved problem-solving transfer (contiguity effect), **strong for high-spatial-ability learners but absent for low-spatial learners**, because high-spatial learners can spare resources to build referential connections between visual and verbal representations — [DeepDyve record](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/doc/0oe7VGwNNA); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Mayer+Sims+For+whom+is+a+picture+worth+a+thousand+words+Extensions+of+a+dual-coding+theory+of+multimedia+learning+1994&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Plass, Chun, Mayer & Leutner (1998), "Supporting visual and verbal learning preferences in a second-language multimedia learning environment", *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 90(1), 25–36, DOI 10.1037/0022-0663.90.1.25: English-speaking German learners read a 762-word German story with optional verbal (translation) and visual (picture/video) annotations; **word translations were remembered better when both visual and verbal annotations had been selected** than one or none; story comprehension was better when learners could use their preferred annotation mode — [Leuphana record](https://fis.leuphana.de/en/publications/supporting-visual-and-verbal-learning-preferences-in-a-second-lan/); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Plass+Chun+Mayer+Leutner+Supporting+visual+and+verbal+learning+preferences+in+a+second-language+multimedia+learning+environment&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Carpenter & Olson (2012), "Are pictures good for learning new vocabulary in a foreign language? Only if you think they are not", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 38(1), 92–101, DOI 10.1037/a0024828: learners studying Swahili words with picture cues vs English-translation cues gave **higher confidence judgements for pictures but did not study them longer**; conflicting results in the literature may be due to learners "overestimating the power of pictures" and thus putting in less effort — [Thesis citing C&O 2012](https://gmwpublic.studenttheses.ub.rug.nl/445/1/Thesis_AF_Aleman.pdf); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Carpenter+Olson+Are+pictures+good+for+learning+new+vocabulary+in+a+foreign+language+Only+if+you+think+they+are+not&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Follow-up (Carpenter and colleagues, *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology*, 2020): when learners experienced only one cue type and then judged which would be more effective, the majority chose pictures regardless of what they had experienced, so metacognitive judgements "are driven more by students' beliefs about learning than by processing fluency" — [Princeton record](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/is-a-picture-really-worth-a-thousand-words-evaluating-contributio/); [OSF](https://osf.io/st4dn)
- Boers, Warren, He & Deconinck (2017), "Does adding pictures to glosses enhance vocabulary uptake from reading?", *System*, 66, 113–129, DOI 10.1016/j.system.2017.03.017: pictures alongside textual glosses "may reduce the amount of attention that L2 readers give to the form of these words" — [UWO record](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/edupub/99); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Boers+Warren+He+Deconinck+Does+adding+pictures+to+glosses+enhance+vocabulary+uptake+from+reading+System+2017&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Bisson, van Heuven, Conklin & Tunney (2013/2015), *PLOS ONE* (eye-tracking of multimodal incidental learning): exposure to FL auditory words with written L1 translations and pictures produced rapid incidental learning; **reading-while-listening promotes both phonological and orthographic form learning and form–meaning links**, whereas spoken-only input is harder and providing orthography helps learners segment auditory forms — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4487591); [Nottingham eprints](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/28438)
- Concreteness effects are robust in paired-associate FL learning: concrete words learned better than abstract at all timepoints in de Groot (2006) and de Groot & Smedinga (2014) [REP] — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/let-the-music-play/C28D533232A39482804924C26DF247DF)

### Inferences
- The picture benefit is conditional: it works when the picture is an *additional* cue integrated with the verbal form, not a *replacement* for the L1 gloss, and when the learner is still forced to retrieve the Japanese form.
- The overconfidence effect is the practical hazard for a self-study app: picture cards "feel" learned, so a self-grading learner will under-review them. Pairing pictures with objective retrieval tests (typed/kana recall) neutralizes this.
- Pictures are most defensible for concrete nouns (where the concreteness effect already helps); for abstract words, verbs of nuance, and function-like words, a picture is likely a seductive detail.

**Design implication for the app:** Use pictures sparingly (concrete nouns only), always alongside the written form and reading, and never let a picture card be graded only by self-judgement — require a form-targeting retrieval step so overconfidence cannot inflate ease ratings.

### Gaps
- No effect sizes retrieved for Plass et al. 1998 or Boers et al. 2017 (abstract-level only).
- No Japanese-specific study on pictures vs kanji/reading retention was located.

## Key Question 4: Audio — does hearing pronunciation at presentation improve retention of form and meaning? Is TTS worth including?

### Takeaway
Hearing the form is strongly supported for learning *phonological* form and form–meaning links (reading-while-listening > reading-only; audio glosses outperform text-only glosses in several studies), and *producing* the word aloud (production effect) gives a durable memory advantage. For Japanese, pitch accent is phonemic and auditory training transfers to new words, so TTS audio at presentation is well justified.

### Cited Findings
- Bisson et al. (eye-tracking / multimodal incidental learning): "reading-while-listening promotes both the learning of phonological and orthographic word forms and form-meaning links"; spoken-only input is harder, and adding orthography helps segment auditory forms — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4487591)
- Icht & Mama (2019), "The effect of vocal production on vocabulary learning in a second language", *Language Teaching Research*, 26(1), 79–98, DOI 10.1177/1362168819883894: Hebrew speakers learned Esperanto words by vocal production vs silent study; **significant production effect at all retention intervals**, with produced words showing *less decay over time*; authors recommend vocalization as "a simple yet effective memory tool in the first stages of L2 learning" — [IUCC record](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-effect-of-vocal-production-on-vocabulary-learning-in-a-second/); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Icht+Mama+The+effect+of+vocal+production+on+vocabulary+learning+in+a+second+language&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- The production effect mechanism: distinctiveness of aloud items at encoding gives an additional retrieval dimension — [Frontiers in Psychology 2014 overview](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00886/text)
- Ludke, Ferreira & Overy (2014), *Memory & Cognition*, 42(1), 41–52, DOI 10.3758/s13421-013-0342-5: listen-and-repeat by singing doubled production/recall of Hungarian phrases relative to speaking them — [PDF](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Ludke-et-al.-2014_SingingForeignLanguage_Mem-Cog.pdf)
- Audio glosses: Iranian EFL study found "audio gloss groups ... and extended audio gloss groups significantly outperformed other groups in vocabulary acquisition in both immediate and delayed retention" — [Irandoc record](https://doaji.irandoc.ac.ir/home/article?id=113056) [SINGLE, lower-tier journal]
- Japanese pitch accent: it is phonemic; a multimodal training study (auditory, visual, gestural) found **pitch-height notation training most robust**, improving perception on trained *and novel* words; another training study found significant improvement in both perception and production that generalized to new words, while controls did not improve — [DOAJ record, Language and Cognition 2024](https://doaj.org/article/643c5707caa449fbb53752a8f14a7d2d); [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/4F3874CC83F8AD938BF5E9EE8654FC7F); [CNY Corridor summary](https://www.cnycorridor.net/publications/multimodal-training-on-l2-japanese-pitch-accent-learning-outcomes-neural-correlates-and-subjective-assessments/)
- Japanese word accent is unpredictable and usually memorized by rote; computer-based repetition exercises (e.g., Speak Everywhere) have been studied for learning word accentuation — [IALLT journal PDF](https://journals.ku.edu/iallt/article/download/8533/8042)
- de Groot (2006) and de Groot & Smedinga (2014) [REP]: phonologically **typical** foreign forms are learned better than atypical ones at all timepoints — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/let-the-music-play/C28D533232A39482804924C26DF247DF)
- A Southampton PhD thesis on audio-supported vocabulary learning reports higher acquisition retained over time with the largest difference in pronunciation — [Southampton eprints](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/434623/) [thesis, not peer-reviewed]

### Inferences
- Audio is low-cost and the evidence is one-directional (helps form learning, no study found showing harm when text is also present). Text + audio together is the modality combination with the best support (Bisson).
- Because pitch accent is lexically contrastive and unpredictable, a Japanese app without audio leaves one dimension of word form unlearnable; TTS with a pitch-accent marker (e.g., visual pitch line, which the multimodal study found most robust) covers both channels.
- A "say it aloud before revealing" prompt converts passive listening into the production effect at no development cost.

**Design implication for the app:** Include TTS (auto-play on card reveal, repeatable), display pitch-accent notation next to the reading, and add a "repeat aloud" prompt before answer reveal; keep audio *on* by default (unlike music).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found that isolates "TTS at presentation vs no audio" for Japanese vocabulary retention with effect sizes.
- Pitch-accent training studies measure perception/production, not vocabulary retention per se.

## Key Question 5: Interaction types — typed recall vs multiple choice vs self-graded flashcards vs cloze vs speaking aloud; best long-term retention per minute; is kana typing via IME good for a Taiwanese learner?

### Takeaway
Retrieval beats restudy (robust), production-format retrieval (typing/writing the form) beats recognition for *productive* and *spelling* knowledge, while recognition formats are adequate and cheaper when only receptive form–meaning links are needed (Nakata 2016). Self-grading is biased toward optimism (Kornell & Bjork), so objective checking is preferable. Within a session, one successful retrieval per item is the most *time-efficient* dose; extra same-session retrievals raise scores mainly by adding time (Nakata 2017). No study directly tests kana-IME typing; the closest evidence favours production formats but warns they slow the learning phase.

### Cited Findings
- Nakata (2016), "Effects of retrieval formats on second language vocabulary learning", *International Review of Applied Linguistics in Language Teaching*, 54(3), 257–289, DOI 10.1515/iral-2015-0022: compared recognition, recall, hybrid, and productive-recall-only conditions; **recall formats are more effective than recognition for acquiring productive knowledge of orthography**; **recognition formats are more desirable when spelling knowledge is not required**; productive recall "may be particularly effective for the acquisition of knowledge of orthography although it may decrease learning phase performance"; for form–meaning connections, recognition formats were found more desirable than recall — [researchmap record](https://researchmap.jp/nakatata/published_papers/735161?lang=ja); [De Gruyter record](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)
- Nakata (2017), "Does repeated practice make perfect? The effects of within-session repeated retrieval on second language vocabulary learning", *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 39(4), 653–679, DOI 10.1017/S0272263116000280: 98 Japanese learners, 16 English–Japanese pairs, 1/3/5/7 retrievals, posttests >2 weeks later; 5 and 7 retrievals scored higher than 1 and 3 at all posttests, **but when time-on-task was controlled, one retrieval produced the largest gain** — additional massed retrievals are an inefficient use of time — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)
- Kang, McDermott & Roediger (2007), "Test format and corrective feedback modify the effect of testing on long-term retention", *European Journal of Cognitive Psychology*, 19(4–5), 528–558 (DOI not retrieved): after reading short articles, initial **short-answer** tests enhanced 3-day recall most; SA practice beat MC practice on a later MC test (d = 0.41) but not significantly on a later SA test; **feedback on SA tests improved final performance, feedback on MC tests did not** — [Gwern-hosted PDF](https://www.gwern.net/docs/spaced-repetition/2007-kang.pdf); [Andy Matuschak notes](https://notes.andymatuschak.org/zTxLkeaWCdBHQYW73o5n6Ka)
- Kornell & Bjork (2007), "The promise and perils of self-regulated study", *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, and Kornell & Bjork (2008), "Optimising self-regulated study: The benefits—and costs—of dropping flashcards", *Memory*, 16(2), 125–136, DOI 10.1080/09658210701763899: learners almost universally drop cards they think they know; dropping "rests on two shaky foundations: students' metacognitive monitoring, and the value they assign to further study"; being allowed to drop had **small but consistently negative effects** on learning — [Bjork Lab PDF 2007](https://sites.lifesci.ucla.edu/psych-bjorklab/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_RBjork_2007.pdf); [Bjork Lab PDF 2008](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_Bjork_2008_Memory.pdf)
- Well-constructed multiple-choice tests can trigger productive retrieval; both MC and cued-recall foster retention of tested information, and MC additionally improves recall of information about the incorrect alternatives (Little & Bjork line of work) — [SAGE 2020 article](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1747021820910694); [Little, Bjork, Bjork & Angello summary](https://itlab.us/TBL/Harvesting/TBL/Multile%20Choice%20Tests%20Exonerated.pdf)
- Typing vs handwriting: EEG study found larger N400 learning effects and higher positive mood for words learned by handwriting than by typing; a 42-adult Arabic-alphabet study found the handwriting group learned letters faster and generalized better; **but a dyslexia subgroup did better with typing**, so demands of handwriting can be a disadvantage for some learners — [PMC Frontiers 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8222525/); [Cerni et al. 2025 Learning & Instruction PDF](https://iris.unife.it/retrieve/6df1edf0-ab98-4533-aa72-8b9758e68344/2025_Cerni_et_al_Learning%26amp%3bInstruction.pdf)
- Production effect (speaking aloud) in L2 vocabulary: durable advantage across intervals (Icht & Mama 2019, see KQ4) — [IUCC record](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-effect-of-vocal-production-on-vocabulary-learning-in-a-second/)
- Barcroft (2002): sentence-writing as an exercise on new words depressed form learning (see KQ2) — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/effects-of-sentence-writing-in-second-language-lexical-acquisitio/)

### Inferences
- Map interaction type to knowledge goal: **recognition/MC** for receptive meaning (cheap, fast, fine for beginners and for kanji-recognition), **typed/kana recall** for productive form and spelling, **speak-aloud** for phonological form. A hybrid schedule (recognition first, then recall once the item is somewhat known) matches Nakata's "hybrid" logic and keeps learning-phase accuracy from collapsing.
- Self-graded flashcards are the weakest *measurement* format (Kornell & Bjork optimism bias); if used, the app should not let the learner remove items on a single "I know it" and should mix in objective checks.
- Per-minute efficiency argues for one successful retrieval per item per session, then spacing across sessions, rather than drilling an item 5–7 times in one sitting.
- For a Taiwanese learner, kana IME typing: the evidence supports *production-format* retrieval for orthography/reading, and typing kana via romaji IME is a legitimate productive recall of the *reading*. However, Nakata notes productive recall slows the learning phase, and IME friction is extra non-learning time. A Taiwanese user already types Chinese via Bopomofo/pinyin IMEs, so IME switching is familiar; the practical cost is keystroke overhead, not conceptual difficulty. No direct evidence exists either way; a reasonable compromise is on-screen kana input (tap kana buttons or romaji-to-kana conversion inside the app) to remove OS-IME switching while preserving productive recall.

**Design implication for the app:** Default review loop = recognition (MC / self-check) for brand-new items → typed-reading recall (in-app romaji→kana, no OS IME switch) once an item has one correct recognition → periodic speak-aloud prompt; grade recall objectively, cap same-session retrievals at one success, and do not let a single self-rated "easy" drop an item.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found on kana-IME typing as a retrieval format for Japanese learners; the typing-vs-handwriting literature concerns alphabetic scripts and N400/EEG outcomes rather than long-term vocabulary retention.
- Kang et al. 2007 DOI not retrieved; a 282-learner French classroom study (writing/typing > multiple choice for spelling/diacritics) appeared in a search snippet but the primary source could not be confirmed, so it is not cited as a finding.
- No "retention per minute of study" comparison across *all* of typed recall, MC, self-graded flashcards and cloze in one experiment; the per-minute conclusion is assembled from Nakata 2016/2017 and Kang et al.

## Key Question 6: Gamification and streaks — streaks, points, leaderboards; which elements have evidence for retention vs just engagement?

### Takeaway
Meta-analyses show small-to-moderate positive effects of gamification on cognitive outcomes (g ≈ .49) that survive rigor filtering, with weaker and less stable effects on motivation (g ≈ .36) and behaviour (g ≈ .25); the elements with the best behavioural evidence are **game fiction/narrative** and **competition combined with collaboration**. Streak evidence comes from Duolingo's internal A/B testing (engagement, not learning outcomes) and is not peer-reviewed; the one peer-reviewed Duolingo study found learning gains smaller than marketing claims but a clear motivational benefit.

### Cited Findings
- Sailer & Homner (2020), "The gamification of learning: A meta-analysis", *Educational Psychology Review*, 32(1), 77–112, DOI 10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w [META]: random-effects effects on **cognitive** outcomes g = .49 [0.30, 0.69], k = 19, N = 1,686; **motivational** g = .36 [0.18, 0.54], k = 16, N = 2,246; **behavioral** g = .25 [0.04, 0.46], k = 9, N = 951. Cognitive effect was **stable in the high-rigor subsplit**; motivational and behavioural effects were less stable. Moderators: **inclusion of game fiction** and **social interaction** significantly moderated behavioural outcomes; game fiction and **combining competition with collaboration** were "particularly effective" for behavioural outcomes — [Augsburg OPUS record](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); [Gamification Research Network summary](https://gamification-research.org/2019/08/does-gamification-of-learning-work/)
- Huang, Ritzhaupt, Sommer, Zhu, Stephen, Valle, Hampton & Li (2020), "The impact of gamification in educational settings on student learning outcomes: A meta-analysis", *Educational Technology Research and Development*, 68(4), 1875–1901, DOI 10.1007/s11423-020-09807-z [META]: integrates quantitative research on gamification in formal education on learning outcomes — [ECNU record](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/the-impact-of-gamification-in-educational-settings-on-student-lea/); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Huang+Ritzhaupt+Sommer+The+impact+of+gamification+in+educational+settings+on+student+learning+outcomes+a+meta-analysis+2020&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page) (overall effect size and moderators not retrieved from the page)
- Loewen, Crowther, Isbell, Kim, Maloney, Miller & Rawal (2019), "Mobile-assisted language learning: A Duolingo case study", *ReCALL*, 31(3), 293–311, DOI 10.1017/S0958344019000065: nine ab initio Turkish learners over a semester (~34 hours); learning gains occurred but "claims made by commercial materials providers may be overestimated", while "the gamification aspect of the app undoubtedly provided a motivational benefit" — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/abs/mobileassisted-language-learning-a-duolingo-case-study/A4D7C8F71782A37D258C19F357DDBCBE)
- Duolingo internal (non-peer-reviewed) evidence: retention team found users reaching a **10-day streak were less likely to drop off**; the streak-saver notification was the first major optimization; >600 streak experiments run; four 2022 retention experiments (mood-changing widget, streak app icons, goal-commitment copy, Perfect Streak Week) made "progress, identity, or commitment more visible at the moment users were likely to act" — [Lazyweb research summary](https://lazyweb.com/research/duolingo-streak-goals-retention); [Jackson Shuttleworth interview summary](https://videohighlight.com/v/_CCwoQZH5hI). These measure engagement/retention of *users*, not retention of *vocabulary*.
- Kornell & Bjork (2008): a design pattern common in gamified apps—letting learners "retire" cards they feel they know—has small but consistent negative effects on learning — [Bjork Lab PDF](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Kornell_Bjork_2008_Memory.pdf)

### Inferences
- Gamification's clearest, most stable benefit is on *learning outcomes* in general (Sailer & Homner cognitive g = .49), but the element-level evidence is thin: points/badges/leaderboards per se were not identified as significant moderators; narrative framing and cooperative-competitive social structures were.
- Streaks are an engagement device with strong industry A/B evidence and no peer-reviewed evidence on vocabulary retention. For a single self-study user they translate into *consistency of spaced practice*, which the spacing literature (KQ7) shows is what actually drives long-term retention; the streak is instrumentally valuable only if it reinforces daily spaced review rather than inflating easy repetitions.
- Leaderboards are irrelevant for a single-user app; streaks + visible progress + a light narrative/goal frame are the cheapest evidence-aligned elements.

**Design implication for the app:** Implement a daily streak tied to *completing the due spaced-review queue* (not raw card count), show progress visibly, optionally add a light goal/narrative frame; skip leaderboards and avoid any "mark as known/retire" shortcut that lets the learner skip reviews.

### Gaps
- Huang et al. 2020's effect sizes and moderator results were not retrieved.
- No peer-reviewed study isolates the effect of streaks on vocabulary retention; all streak evidence is proprietary engagement data.

## Key Question 7: Session structure — session length, breaks, time of day, sleep and consolidation

### Takeaway
Spacing across sessions is the single most robust structural variable (meta-analytic medium-to-large effect; longer gaps better for delayed retention; equal vs expanding gaps equivalent). Sleep after learning consolidates new word forms into the lexicon (Dumay & Gaskell) and, when placed *between* two sessions, halves the relearning needed and improves 1-week and 6-month retention (Mazza et al. 2016). Direct evidence on optimal session *length* was not found; the within-session data (Nakata 2017) favour short sessions with one retrieval per item.

### Cited Findings
- Kim & Webb (2022), "The effects of spaced practice on second language learning: A meta-analysis", *Language Learning*, 72(1), 269–319, DOI 10.1111/lang.12479 [META]: 37 experimental studies; spacing had a **medium-to-large effect** on L2 learning; **shorter spacing was as effective as longer spacing on immediate posttests but less effective on delayed posttests**; **equal and expanding spacing were statistically equivalent**; effect-size variability explained by learning target, number of sessions, type of practice, activity type, feedback timing, and retention interval — [SSLLT paper citing Kim & Webb](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/download/35602/30649/76142); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Kim+Webb+The+effects+of+spaced+practice+on+second+language+learning+A+meta-analysis+Language+Learning+2022&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Nakata (2015), *SSLA*, 37(4), 677–711, DOI 10.1017/S0272263114000825: expanding vs equal spacing in L2 vocabulary; spaced flashcards retained vocabulary at 2–3× the rate of massed study — [Search summary](https://flashcards.world/wiki/learn-a-new-language-with-flashcards/)
- Nakata (2017), *SSLA*, 39(4), 653–679, DOI 10.1017/S0272263116000280: within one session, one retrieval per item was most efficient once time-on-task was controlled (see KQ5) — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E)
- Dumay & Gaskell (2007), "Sleep-associated changes in the mental representation of spoken words", *Psychological Science*, 18(1), 35–39, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01845.x: new spoken word forms are acquired quickly but only begin to compete lexically with similar-sounding known words after a consolidation period that depends on sleep; words learned at 8 p.m. showed competition effects after 12 h (including sleep) and at 24 h; words learned at 8 a.m. showed none after 12 h awake, only at 24 h after sleep — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/7437); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Dumay+Gaskell+Sleep-associated+changes+in+the+mental+representation+of+spoken+words+Psychological+Science+2007&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Lindsay & Gaskell, "Lexical integration of novel words without sleep" (JEP:LMC) [CONTESTED nuance]: shows that under some training regimes lexical integration can occur without sleep, so sleep is a strong but not strictly necessary consolidation route — [ResearchGate PDF](https://researchgate.net/profile/Shane-Lindsay/publication/229007079_Lexical_Integration_of_Novel_Words_Without_Sleep/links/54d0e1070cf29ca81103f70e/Lexical-Integration-of-Novel-Words-Without-Sleep.pdf)
- Mazza, Gerbier, Gustin, Maslova, Chadwick, Barrault & Magnin? (2016), "Relearn faster and retain longer: Along with practice, sleep makes perfect", *Psychological Science*, 27(10), 1321–1330, DOI 10.1177/0956797616659930: 40 participants, two sessions 12 h apart; one group learned in the morning and relearned the same evening, the other learned in the evening, slept, and relearned next morning; **sleep between sessions halved the relearning needed and produced better retention at 1 week and 6 months** — [APS abstract page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797616659930/); [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Mazza+Gerbier+Relearn+faster+and+retain+longer+along+with+practice+sleep+makes+perfect+Psychological+Science+2016&rows=1&select=DOI,title,container-title,issued,volume,page)
- Sleep-dependent consolidation summary: English–German word-pair learning was better when sleep occurred within a few hours of encoding, and significantly worse when >14 h of daytime wake separated learning and recall; a daytime nap after learning word pairs also improved retention vs staying awake — [Merrimack review "Sleep and Word Learning"](https://www.merrimack.edu/wp-content/uploads/Sleep-and-Word-Learning.pdf); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4568164)
- Children: sleeping soon after learning had long-lasting memory benefits, especially for children with weaker vocabulary — [White Rose eprints](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/160724/)
- Non-peer-reviewed Duolingo data on best time of day exists but is correlational engagement data — [WEF summary of Duolingo data](https://weforum.org/stories/2018/02/the-best-time-of-day-to-learn-a-new-language-according-duolingo-data)

### Inferences
- The structural levers with the strongest evidence are: (1) spread learning across days (spacing), (2) keep each item to one successful retrieval per session, and (3) ensure sleep follows new learning before the next review — an evening new-word session followed by a next-morning review is the regime Mazza et al. tested.
- Expanding-interval schedules are not better than equal intervals; a simple SRS with roughly fixed multipliers is fine.
- Optimal absolute session length is not established; Nakata's efficiency result implies short sessions that stop once each due item has been retrieved once.

**Design implication for the app:** Build around a daily spaced-review queue (equal or expanding intervals both fine), schedule *new* items late in the day with their first review the next morning, stop a session once each due item has one successful retrieval, and avoid same-session re-drilling of already-correct items.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study found that directly compares session lengths (e.g., 10 vs 30 min) or break schedules for L2 vocabulary.
- Time-of-day effects on *encoding quality* (independent of sleep timing) were not isolated in the sources found; the morning-vs-evening results are driven by sleep proximity.
- Mazza et al. 2016 author list beyond the first two was not verified; the effect sizes were not reported in the abstract retrieved.
