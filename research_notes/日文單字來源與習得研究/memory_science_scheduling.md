# Memory science for L2 vocabulary scheduling and practice format (spacing, retrieval, interleaving, session size, direction, order, feedback, difficulty)

Scope note: findings are tagged **[L2 vocab]** when the study used real second-language word pairs (Swahili–English, English–Japanese, French–English, Spanish, etc.) and **[general PA]** when they used general paired-associate or non-language material. L2-vocabulary paired-associate flashcard studies are close enough to a Japanese vocabulary web app that both categories are directly usable; the distinction is noted so the report writer can weight them.

Research date: 2026-10-07.

---

## Key Question 1 — Spacing: optimal intervals, and expanding vs uniform spacing

### Takeaway
Spacing beats massing robustly (meta-analytic recall 47% vs 37% in general verbal tasks; "medium-to-large" effect in 48 L2 experiments). The optimal gap scales with how long you want to remember: roughly 10–20% of the retention interval for weeks-to-months horizons, falling to ~5% for a 1-year horizon. Expanding schedules are **not** reliably better than equal-interval schedules once absolute spacing is held constant; what matters is that the *first* retrieval is delayed enough to be effortful, and that total spacing is long.

### Cited Findings
- **[general PA] Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer (2006)**, *Psychological Bulletin*, 132(3), 354–380. Meta-analysis of 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments from 184 articles (254 studies). Spaced study recalled 47% vs massed 37%. The inter-study interval (ISI) producing maximal retention *increased* as the retention interval increased. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/); [Author PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaPashlerVulWixtedRohrer-PB-2006.pdf); [York U summary](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CPVWR2006.html)
- **[general PA] Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008)**, "Spacing effects in learning: A temporal ridgeline of optimal retention", *Psychological Science*, 19(11), 1095–1102. >1,350 participants learned facts, reviewed after a gap of up to 3.5 months, final test up to 1 year later. At any retention interval (RI), increasing the gap first increased, then decreased, final performance; the optimal gap grew with RI. Expressed as a proportion of RI, the optimal gap fell from ~20–40% of a 1-week RI to ~5–10% of a 1-year RI (the authors' site states ~20% for RIs of a few weeks, ~5% for 1 year). — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [Author PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf); [York U summary](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/CVRWP2008.html)
- **[L2 vocab] Kim & Webb (2022)**, "The effects of spaced practice on second language learning: A meta-analysis", *Language Learning*, 72(1), 269–319, DOI 10.1111/lang.12479. 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments (N = 3,411). Spacing had a medium-to-large effect on L2 learning; shorter spacing was as effective as longer spacing on immediate posttests but *less* effective on delayed posttests; **equal and expanding spacing were statistically equivalent**; effect-size variability was explained by learning target, number of sessions, type of practice, activity type, feedback timing and retention interval. — [SSLLT summary (Rogers)](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/49487); [PolyU repository (Rogers, Optimizing Distributed Practice)](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/113055/1/Rogers_Optimizing_Distributed_Practice.pdf); [Semantic Scholar record](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1111/lang.12479?fields=title,year,venue,citationCount)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata (2015)**, "Effects of expanding and equal spacing on second language vocabulary learning: Does gradually increasing spacing increase vocabulary learning?", *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 37(4), 677–711, DOI 10.1017/S0272263114000825. Controlled absolute spacing between expanding and equal conditions (earlier studies had confounded it). Under lenient scoring, expanding spacing gave a small but significant advantage (+4.6% score); under strict scoring there was no significant difference. — [SSLA vol 37(4) front matter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/09BC34DFB66B6264F87B4A30D5CDF543/S0272263115000406a.pdf/sla-volume-37-issue-4-cover-and-front-matter.pdf); [Secondary summary](https://sanad.iau.ir/en/Article/897069)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata's PhD dissertation (Victoria University of Wellington, 2013)**, "Optimising Second Language Vocabulary Learning from Flashcards": "Study 3 found no significant difference between equal and expanding spacing"; massed learning gave the best performance *during* practice but the worst on delayed tests; greater spacing between repetitions mattered more than block size. — [LINGUIST List 24.3797](https://linguistlist.org/issues/24/3797)
- **[general PA / vocabulary word pairs] Karpicke & Roediger (2007)**, "Expanding retrieval practice promotes short-term retention, but equally spaced retrieval enhances long-term retention", *JEP: Learning, Memory & Cognition*. Expanding retrieval of vocabulary word pairs gave a short-term advantage at 10 min; equally spaced retrieval gave superior retention at 2 days, with or without feedback. Delaying the *first* test improved long-term retention regardless of later spacing; expanding the interval between repeated tests had little effect on long-term retention across 3 experiments. — [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expanding-retrieval-practice-promotes-short-term-retention-but-eq/); [PDF mirror](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2007-karpicke.pdf)
- **[general PA, GRE-type word pairs] Kornell (2009)**, "Optimising learning using flashcards: Spacing is more effective than cramming", *Applied Cognitive Psychology*, 23(9), 1297–1317. Participants learned some pairs as 4 piles of 5 cards (massed/small stacks) and others as 1 pile of 20 cards (spaced). In one experiment massed scored 38% vs spaced 65% with identical study time. Larger stacks yielded more learning for 90% of students, yet 72% *preferred* the small stacks even after trying both. — [CiNii record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152); [Learning & the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/flashcard-strategies/)
- **[L2 vocab, classroom] Rogers & Cheung (2020)**, "Input spacing and the learning of L2 vocabulary in a classroom context", *Language Teaching Research*, 24(5), 616–641, DOI 10.1177/1362168818805251. 52 Hong Kong primary pupils (L1 Cantonese) studied 20 English adjectives in two episodes separated by 1 day vs 8 days, multiple-choice posttest after 4 weeks. The **1-day (shorter) gap won**, contrary to lab findings; the authors suggest lag effects may be attenuated by age, context and teaching procedure. — [EdUHK repository](https://repository.eduhk.hk/en/publications/input-spacing-and-the-learning-of-l2-vocabulary-in-a-classroom-co/)
- **[L2 vocab, Spanish, middle school] Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer (2014)**, "Improving students' long-term knowledge retention through personalized review", *Psychological Science*, 25(3), 639–647, DOI 10.1177/0956797613504302. 179 students used a retrieval-practice system (COLT) over a semester on 10 chapters of Spanish vocabulary. On a time-matched cumulative exam, personalised (model-based) spaced review beat massed study by 16.5% and beat a one-size-fits-all spaced schedule by 10.0%; gains were largest for early-semester material (~two letter grades). — [Author PDF](https://home.cs.colorado.edu/~mozer/Research/Selected%20Publications/reprints/LindseyShroyerPashlerMozer2014Published.pdf); [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797613504302); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/01/140121093046.htm)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata (2008)**, "English vocabulary learning with word lists, word cards and computers: implications from cognitive psychology research for optimal spaced learning", *ReCALL*, 20(1), 3–20. 226 Japanese high-school students learned 10 English words; the computer (spaced) group significantly outperformed the list group on the delayed posttest; the card group fell in between. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/english-vocabulary-learning-with-word-lists-word-cards-and-computers-implications-from-cognitive-psychology-research-for-optimal-spaced-learning/4742121ED692165551C531F6464E67FA)

### Inferences
- For an app, "expanding vs uniform" is a second-order choice; a scheduler that (a) delays the first review enough to make it effortful, (b) grows gaps with the desired retention horizon, and (c) adapts per item/learner (as in Lindsey et al. 2014) captures nearly all the available benefit. Standard SM-2/FSRS-style expanding schedules are therefore defensible, but the app should not promise that expansion per se is "scientifically optimal".
- Cepeda 2008's ratios give a rule of thumb: if the user wants a word to survive ~1 month, the key review gap is ~3–6 days; for ~1 year, ~2–4 weeks. These are between-session gaps for a single review, not full multi-review schedules.
- Rogers & Cheung (2020) is a reminder that very long lags can hurt young or low-proficiency learners who forget before the second exposure; initial lags for brand-new words should be short (same day or next day), then grow.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the actual Hedges' g values from Kim & Webb (2022) (abstract paywalled; Semantic Scholar returned no abstract). Only "medium-to-large" is confirmed.
- Nakata (2015) exact sample size and conditions were not visible; only the +4.6% lenient-scoring result and the strict-scoring null were reported by secondary sources.

## Key Question 2 — Retrieval practice / testing effect; recall vs recognition; transfer between formats

### Takeaway
Retrieval (actively recalling) is the single most important practice ingredient: once a word pair has been recalled correctly, *further study without testing adds nothing* to 1-week retention, while repeated testing adds a large amount (Karpicke & Roediger 2008, Swahili–English). For L2 word pairs, recall formats build spelling/productive knowledge; recognition (multiple-choice) formats are as good or better when spelling is not required (Nakata 2016). Within one session, 5–7 retrievals beat 1–3, but per minute of study, a single retrieval is the most efficient (Nakata 2017).

### Cited Findings
- **[L2 vocab] Karpicke & Roediger (2008)**, "The critical importance of retrieval for learning", *Science*, 319(5865), 966–968. 40 undergraduates learned 40 Swahili–English pairs via computerised flashcards; final test 1 week later. Conditions: standard repeated study+test; once recalled, dropped from test but repeatedly studied; dropped from study but repeatedly tested; dropped from both. Repeated studying after an item was learned had **no effect** on 1-week recall; repeated testing produced a large positive effect. Students' predictions of their own performance were uncorrelated with actual performance. — [Supporting material (Purdue)](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2008/2008_Karpicke_Roediger_ScienceSupportingMaterial.pdf); [Purdue press release](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html); [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-critical-importance-of-retrieval-for-learning/)
- **[general, prose] Roediger & Karpicke (2006)**, "Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention", *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. After 5 min, restudy beat testing; at 2 days and 1 week, testing beat restudy. Secondary sources report the 1-week figures as ~56% (tested) vs ~40% (restudy); another summary reports 61% vs 40% for a different experiment/condition. — [PDF (ECU)](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf); [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/test-enhanced-learning); [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-taking-memory-tests-improves-long-term-ret/)
- **[L2 vocab] Barcroft (2007)**, "Effects of opportunities for word retrieval during second language vocabulary learning", *Language Learning*, 57(1). Spanish-speaking intermediate learners of English read a text with five target words appearing 3 times each; the retrieval group saw the L1 gloss only on the first occurrence and had to retrieve on the next two. Secondary sources report a ~51% increase in vocabulary learning for the retrieval group over control. — [Secondary summary (ILS)](https://www.ils.unimas.my/images/pdf/ilsv2n1_radina_deli.pdf); [DOAJ record citing it](https://doaj.org/article/d7a8c6ce404b47f8b73691685b1b9068)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata (2016)**, "Effects of retrieval formats on second language vocabulary learning", *IRAL*, 54(3), 257–289, DOI 10.1515/iral-2015-0022. 64 English-speaking college students learned 60 Swahili–English pairs via flashcard software under recognition, recall, hybrid (recall+recognition), or productive-recall-only formats. Results: (a) recall formats are more effective than recognition for acquiring **productive knowledge of orthography (spelling)**; (b) **recognition formats are more desirable than recall when knowledge of spelling is not required** (recognition was as effective or more effective for form–meaning connection and took less time). — [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en); [researchmap](https://researchmap.jp/nakatata/published_papers/735161?lang=ja)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata dissertation (2013)**: "Recognition formats proved more effective for acquiring form-meaning connections. Productive recall was particularly beneficial for orthographic knowledge acquisition, despite reducing learning phase performance." — [LINGUIST List 24.3797](https://linguistlist.org/issues/24/3797)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata (2017)**, "Does repeated practice make perfect? The effects of within-session repeated retrieval on second language vocabulary learning", *SSLA*, 39(4), 653–679, DOI 10.1017/S0272263116000280. 98 Japanese learners retrieved 16 English–Japanese pairs 1, 3, 5 or 7 times in one session; delayed posttest >2 weeks later. 5 and 7 retrievals significantly beat 1 and 3; but **when time-on-task was controlled, 1 retrieval gave the largest gain per unit time**. The dissertation version adds that the benefit of ~5 retrievals persisted 4 weeks after treatment. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-repeated-practice-make-perfect-the-effects-of-withinsession-repeated-retrieval-on-second-language-vocabulary-learning/F14BA8A576CD2563D14CEA46E35D842E); [LINGUIST List](https://linguistlist.org/issues/24/3797)
- **[general PA] Rawson & Dunlosky (2011)**, "Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice for durable and efficient learning: How much is enough?", *JEP: General*, 140(3), 283–302. Recommend an initial criterion of **3 correct recalls** in the first session, then **3 relearning sessions** at widely spaced intervals; initial-session recall count had strong early effects that weakened as relearning increased, and relearning had pronounced long-term benefits at minimal extra cost. — [Kent State lab publications](https://www.kent.edu/psychology/comprehension-memory-lab/rawsons-comprehension-memory-lab-publications); [retrievalpractice.org summary](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning)
- **[review] Nation (2013), *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language*, Ch. 11 "Deliberate vocabulary learning from word cards"**: "the key to word card use is for learners to look at the L2 word or L1 meaning on one side, and test themselves to see if they can recall" the other side (quoted via Lei & Reynolds 2022 research synthesis). — [PMC9485613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9485613/); [Cambridge chapter page](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/learning-vocabulary-in-another-language/deliberate-vocabulary-learning-from-word-cards/74EB1B4830C8A6C6A9E1490BCA444AD3)

### Inferences
- App rule: every exposure after the first should be a retrieval attempt, not a "show both sides" study card. Restudy after successful recall is wasted time (Karpicke & Roediger 2008).
- Format choice should follow the target skill: if the user only needs to *read* Japanese (recognise kanji/kana word → meaning), multiple-choice or self-graded recognition is efficient and faster per item (Nakata 2016). If the user needs to *write/type* the word, require typed recall.
- A "learned" criterion of 1 correct recall in the first session (efficient per Nakata 2017) or up to 3 (Rawson & Dunlosky 2011) is supportable; 5+ within a single session is better in absolute terms but not time-efficient. Reasonable default: 1–2 successful retrievals in the introduction session, then rely on spaced relearning sessions.

### Gaps
- Direct evidence on *transfer* between formats (does MC practice improve later free recall and vice versa) specifically for L2 vocabulary is thin in what I found; Nakata 2016 shows recall formats help spelling and recognition formats suffice for meaning, which is partial evidence. No L2-specific meta-analysis of recognition→recall transfer was located.
- Barcroft 2007 figures come from secondary citations, not the original abstract.

## Key Question 3 — Interleaving vs blocking; semantic clustering

### Takeaway
The interference problem is real but subtler than the classic claim: learning semantically related words together (colours, fruits, days) increases confusion/interference errors and slows learning; in the best-controlled recent study it did not lower overall posttest accuracy, and spacing helped *unrelated* words more than related ones. Older studies (Tinkham 1993; Waring 1997; Erten & Tekin 2008) found clear harm; a few later studies found the opposite, so the field is "mixed but leaning against semantic sets". Random mixing of unrelated words (what the user calls "random" presentation) is the safe default.

### Cited Findings
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata & Suzuki (2019)**, "Effects of massing and spacing on the learning of semantically related and unrelated words", *SSLA*, 41(2), 287–311, DOI 10.1017/S0272263118000219. 133 Japanese university students (massed 66, spaced 56; 62/54 analysed) learned 48 English–Japanese pairs, half semantically related. 1-week delayed posttest: related massed 17.74%, related spaced 24.15%, unrelated massed 15.99%, **unrelated spaced 29.09%**. Interference (within-set) errors on the delayed test: massed-related 6.32%, spaced-related 3.70%, massed-unrelated 0.47%. No significant overall difference between related and unrelated items, but related items produced more interference errors, and spacing benefited unrelated items more than related ones. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10); [PDF](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/F58BA8D70385603B9C42E408BFCB8A10/S0272263118000219a.pdf/effects-of-massing-and-spacing-on-the-learning-of-semantically-related-and-unrelated-words.pdf)
- **[L2 vocab] Tinkham (1993)**: participants needed significantly more trials to learn semantically related sets (e.g., apple, apricot) than unrelated sets (e.g., mouse, sky). **Waring (1997)** replicated and framed it as interference theory: words learned at the same time that share too many semantic features interfere with each other and impair retention. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022 review](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.997951/full); [Auburn thesis summarising](https://etd.auburn.edu//bitstream/10415/2730/2/Category%20Effects%20of%20Vocabulary%20Presentation%20in%20L2%20Vocabulary%20Acquisition%20in%20Novice.pdf)
- **[L2 vocab, children] Erten & Tekin (2008)**, "Effects on vocabulary acquisition of presenting new words in semantic sets versus semantically unrelated sets", *System*, 36(3), 407–422. 60 fourth-graders taught 80 words in semantic vs unrelated sets; unrelated sets yielded better recall and the difference persisted long-term; test completion time was much longer for semantically related items (slower recall). — [COMU repository](https://acikerisim.comu.edu.tr/items/7a90a159-d648-49ff-aabb-94a7b47c4a5e/full)
- **Contested**: a 2022 review notes that "recent studies have yielded mixed results, with four studies finding that semantic clustering resulted in significantly higher posttest scores". — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.997951/full)
- **[L2 vocab] Schneider, Healy & Bourne (2002)**, "What is learned under difficult conditions is hard to forget: Contextual interference effects in foreign vocabulary acquisition, retention, and transfer", *Journal of Memory and Language*, 46(2), 419–440, DOI 10.1006/jmla.2001.2813 (207 citations per Semantic Scholar). Harder training conditions (contextual interference, L1→L2 direction) hurt immediate performance but reduced forgetting on a 1-week delayed test. — [CU Boulder record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_52638); [2025 replication/extension, PMC12108878](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12108878/)

### Inferences
- For the app: do **not** group new words by semantic field in the same introduction session (e.g., don't introduce 青・赤・緑・黄 together). Draw new words from a frequency-ordered pool but shuffle so that near-synonyms, antonyms and same-category items are separated by at least a session. Thematic/story-based grouping (words that co-occur in a scene but are not of the same semantic class) is not covered by the interference finding and is generally reported as neutral-to-positive.
- "Random" order of mixed words is itself a form of interleaving and is consistent with the spacing data: Nakata & Suzuki's spaced-unrelated condition (29%) was the best of the four cells.
- Related-word confusion errors (6.3% massed vs 3.7% spaced) suggest that if related words must be learned, spacing them reduces, but does not eliminate, the confusion.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of semantic clustering in L2 vocabulary with a pooled effect size was found; the evidence is individual studies with mixed directions.
- The Schneider et al. (2002) abstract was paywalled; direction-of-effect comes from secondary descriptions (CU Boulder pages and a 2025 replication), not the original numbers.

## Key Question 4 — How many new words per day/session; set size; finite pool vs unlimited; coverage thresholds (incl. Japanese)

### Takeaway
The literature gives no validated "N new words per day"; the one number with real evidence is the ratio of new items to reviews (Anki's default 20 new : 200 reviews, i.e. roughly 1:10, is based on observed workload accumulation, not a controlled study). Set/block size per se barely matters once spacing is equalised (Nakata & Webb 2016); the limiting factor is cumulative review load. For a *finite, mastery-based* pool, coverage research gives the targets: 95% coverage for assisted comprehension, 98% for unassisted/independent reading (English: 8,000–9,000 word families for written text, 6,000–7,000 for spoken). For Japanese, Matsushita's General Learners' Vocabulary List is organised in five levels up to 10,000 words, and Matsushita (2014) argues 93% coverage suffices with dictionary help.

### Cited Findings
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata & Webb (2016)**, "Does studying vocabulary in smaller sets increase learning? The effects of part and whole learning on second language vocabulary acquisition", *SSLA*, 38(3), 523–552, DOI 10.1017/S0272263115000236. Compared 20-item whole learning vs 4-item and 10-item part learning (Experiment 2 also manipulated spacing). "Spacing has a larger effect on learning than the part-whole distinction": large blocks beat small ones only when spacing was confounded; with equal spacing, no significant block-size difference. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-studying-vocabulary-in-smaller-sets-increase-learning/E17B75ABAE1300734AF014C363D59FBC); [LINGUIST List dissertation summary](https://linguistlist.org/issues/24/3797)
- **[general PA] Kornell (2009)**: 1 stack of 20 cards beat 4 stacks of 5 (65% vs 38% in one experiment) with time held constant — i.e., larger within-session sets are better because they *increase* the lag between repetitions of the same card. — [CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152); [summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/flashcard-strategies/)
- **[community/software convention] Anki**: default 20 new cards/day and 200 max reviews/day; the manual says that with 20 new/day, early-stage daily reviews build up to ~200/day, and that the new-card limit "should typically be at least 10x smaller than your review limit". — [Anki manual: deck options](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html); [Migaku guide](https://migaku.com/blog/language-fun/anki-settings-for-language-learning)
- **[software/FSRS] Desired retention**: FSRS defaults to 0.9 (90%) target recall; reviews grow "almost exponentially" at higher retention; Anki's "compute minimum recommended retention" estimates the retention that minimises workload per unit of knowledge, and users commonly report it lands in the 0.80–0.85 range. (Forum/user evidence, not peer-reviewed.) — [Anki forums CMRR thread](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/about-minimum-recommended-retention-cmrr/54501/2); [FSRS tuning guide](https://jiten.moe/guides/tuning-fsrs); [Anki forums workload-vs-DR](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/replace-cmrr-with-workload-vs-dr-graph-more/63234?page=2)
- **[coverage, English] Nation (2006)**, "How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?", *Canadian Modern Language Review*, 63(1). If 98% coverage is needed for unassisted comprehension, 8,000–9,000 word families are needed for written text and 6,000–7,000 for spoken text (BNC-based lists; ~200,000-word spoken corpus analysed). — [VUW PDF](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf); [Replication: Schmitt, Cobb et al. 2017, Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)
- **[coverage, general]** 95% of running words = threshold for adequate (assisted) comprehension; 98%+ ideal if incidental learning from reading is desired (Nation 2013). — [JALT JER article on Japanese vocabulary levels test](https://jalt-publications.org/content/index.php/jer/article/view/1221)
- **[coverage, Japanese] Matsushita (2014)** claims learners should know **93%** of running words for adequate comprehension *with some dictionary help*; the proportion for incidental learning in Japanese is "unknown". — [JALT JER: Developing a Japanese Vocabulary Levels Test for extensive reading](https://jalt-publications.org/content/index.php/jer/article/view/1221)
- **[Japanese word lists] Matsushita (2012a) General Learners' Vocabulary List** is classified into five levels: 2,000, 4,000, 6,000, 8,000 and 10,000 words; its text coverage was high both for native-speaker text and learner text, and for spoken as well as written Japanese. A Japanese Vocabulary Levels Test was built on it, expanded from a 2,000-word version to cover the 5,000 most frequent words (KR-21 = .98). — [JALT JER article](https://jalt-publications.org/content/index.php/jer/article/view/1221); [JALT TLT 44.5](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/44.5tlt-art1.pdf)
- **[Japanese, JLPT-scale]** A NINJAL open-house summary of Matsushita's vocabulary-profiler work gives rough requirements: ~1,500–2,000 words for basic (N5/N4), ~7,000 for intermediate (N3/N2), 18,000+ for advanced (N1); and states 95% coverage = "understood to some degree", 98% = "can enjoy reading independently". (Non-peer-reviewed summary; treat as indicative.) — [NINJAL openhouse 2022 PDF](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/openhouse/2022/pdf/23.pdf); [Matsushita Text Covering Efficiency talk](https://linguistics.pitt.edu/event/optimizing-vocabulary-learning-through-text-covering-efficiency-and-word-tier-analysis)
- **[review] Lei & Reynolds (2022)**, "Learning English vocabulary from word cards: A research synthesis" (PMC9485613): word-card studies within a single day showed large effects; spaced conditions over >1 day showed mixed small-to-large effects; cites Pimsleur's (1967) memory schedule of 5 s, 25 s, 2 min, 10 min, ... (×5 each step) as the classic expanding template. — [PMC9485613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9485613/)

### Inferences
- "How many new words per day" should be derived from a review-budget constraint, not a fixed number: new_per_day ≈ (daily review budget in cards) / 10 is the only empirically grounded heuristic (Anki workload data), and it matches the spacing literature's requirement that each word gets several spaced retrievals.
- Set size within a session can be large (20+ items) with no penalty and some benefit from longer within-session lags (Kornell 2009; Nakata & Webb 2016). Tiny 4–5-item drill blocks are the thing to avoid.
- A finite, frequency-ordered pool (Matsushita's 2,000/4,000/6,000/8,000/10,000 bands) is well supported as a target structure: coverage research is explicitly about *finite* frequency bands, and mastery of a band maps to a comprehension threshold. "Unlimited" pools have no supporting literature; the coverage curve flattens sharply after ~10,000 words, so marginal value per word declines.
- For a Japanese app aimed at reading, a 5,000–10,000-word pool with the 93% (Matsushita) / 95% / 98% coverage milestones as progress markers is a defensible design.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests "X new words per day" ceilings directly; the 20/day figure is a software default with workload rationale only.
- Nation's often-cited practical pack-size advice (smaller packs for harder words, larger for easy) could not be retrieved verbatim; only the principle "ensure the number of words in the pack suits the difficulty of the words" was found in a secondary source ([PASAA/Chula PDF](https://www.culi.chula.ac.th/Images/asset/pasaa_journal/file-12-91-rmg48v810783.pdf)). Exact numbers are not confirmed.
- Matsushita's exact coverage-by-vocabulary-size curve for Japanese (e.g., words needed for 95%/98%) was not retrieved; the JALT PDFs were binary and un-parseable. A secondary search summary mentioned "10,000 words ≈ 86% coverage" but the source could not be verified, so it is **not** reported as a finding.
- No study on "learning burden" quantifying extra reviews needed for kanji vs kana words was found in this pass.

## Key Question 5 — Direction of learning: L2→L1 (receptive) vs L1→L2 (productive)

### Takeaway
Direction determines what you get: practising L2→L1 builds recognition, practising L1→L2 builds production, with limited cross-over (Webb 2009). L1→L2 is harder during training but forgets less over a week (Schneider, Healy & Bourne 2002) and is what gives spelling/orthographic knowledge (Nakata 2016). Practical consensus: start receptive for fast gains, then add productive retrieval for items the learner needs to produce.

### Cited Findings
- **[L2 vocab] Webb (2009)**, "The effects of receptive and productive learning of word pairs on vocabulary knowledge", *RELC Journal*, 40(3). Reception defined as L2 (English)→L1 (Japanese) translation; production as L1→L2. On a productive test, productive learners had a sizable advantage; on recognition tests, receptive learners outperformed productive learners. Productive learning of word pairs is more effective for productive knowledge; receptive learning for receptive knowledge. — [Secondary summary (ensani.ir)](https://ensani.ir/fa/article/download/415320); [VUW repository](https://ir.wgtn.ac.nz/handle/123456789/23701?show=full)
- **[L2 vocab] Schneider, Healy & Bourne (2002)**, *JML*, 46(2), 419–440, DOI 10.1006/jmla.2001.2813. Native English speakers studied English–French pairs. French→English (L2→L1) did better on the immediate test (easier to map new form onto known meaning); one week later the English→French (L1→L2, harder) group showed better retention. — [CU Boulder record](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_52638); [PMC12108878 (2025 extension)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12108878/)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata (2016)**: recall (productive) formats are more effective for productive knowledge of orthography; recognition suffices when spelling is not required. — [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iral-2015-0022/html?lang=en)
- **[review] Nation (2013)** via Lei & Reynolds (2022): "looking at the L1 and trying to recall the L2 form involves productive knowledge of form", "looking at the L2 and trying to recall the L1 meaning involves receptive knowledge of meaning" — the two sides train different knowledge. — [PMC9485613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9485613/)

### Inferences
- App rule: default card direction should match the user's goal. For a reading-focused Japanese app: Japanese word → meaning (receptive) first; introduce meaning → Japanese (typed or kana-input) cards only for words flagged for active use, or as a later "promotion" stage.
- Because L1→L2 is harder and shows less forgetting, scheduling it with *longer* intervals once learned is consistent with the desirable-difficulty literature; but expect lower in-session success rates, so the first-session criterion should be lenient for productive cards.

### Gaps
- Schneider et al. (2002) numerical results (percent recalled per direction at 1 week) were not obtainable (paywall, abstract withheld on Semantic Scholar).

## Key Question 6 — Random vs fixed presentation order

### Takeaway
The direct L2-vocabulary evidence on randomised order within a list is thin; what exists is the robust serial-position effect (primacy/recency in fixed lists) and Nation's/Nakata's long-standing advice to change card order so learners don't use position as a cue. Random order with sufficient within-session lag is consistent with all the spacing findings (Kornell 2009; Nakata & Webb 2016); no study found that fixed order helps.

### Cited Findings
- **[general memory]** Serial position effect: superior recall for items at the start (primacy — more rehearsal) and end (recency — less interference) of a list; middle items are worst. — [Macmillan cognition text](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/cogtk2/serial_position_effect.html); [JoVE](https://coach.jove.com/introduction-to-psychology/memory-explained/what-is-serial-position-effect)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata (2008)**: word lists performed worst on the delayed posttest versus cards and computer-scheduled study; list learners' recall for 4 of 10 items was significantly lower. The study's rationale is that lists fix item position and prevent spacing/retrieval control. — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/recall/article/english-vocabulary-learning-with-word-lists-word-cards-and-computers-implications-from-cognitive-psychology-research-for-optimal-spaced-learning/4742121ED692165551C531F6464E67FA)
- **[general PA] Kornell (2009)** and **[L2 vocab] Nakata & Webb (2016)**: the mechanism by which larger, shuffled stacks beat small fixed sub-stacks is the longer lag between repetitions of each card, not order per se. — [CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1361699994055985152); [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/does-studying-vocabulary-in-smaller-sets-increase-learning/E17B75ABAE1300734AF014C363D59FBC)
- **[practice guidance]** Nation's word-card guidelines (summarised in a 2022 synthesis) include keeping cards simple, using L1 meaning on the back, promoting retrieval, and the general advice to shuffle cards; the synthesis contains "no specific guidance on pack sizes". — [PMC9485613](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9485613/)

### Inferences
- Randomising order within a session is low-risk and recommended: it removes position cues (serial-position effect), and combined with a minimum within-session lag (e.g., ≥N other cards between repeats of the same word) it reproduces the conditions of the "large stack" winner in Kornell (2009).
- Pure random sampling across the *whole* pool each session would violate spacing (some words would be seen too often, others too rarely). The scheduler should pick *which* words are due by spacing/retention model, then shuffle *that* due set.
- Avoid presenting a word in the same adjacent-pair context repeatedly (e.g., always after the same neighbour), since neighbour cues act like list-position cues.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled L2-vocabulary experiment isolating "random vs fixed order with spacing equalised"; the recommendation is inferred from serial-position theory plus the list-vs-card results. Nakata (2011), "Computer-assisted second language vocabulary learning in a paired-associate paradigm: a critical investigation of flashcard software" (*Computer Assisted Language Learning*, 24(1)) is the standard source that lists software-design criteria including order control, but I could not retrieve its abstract this session to quote the criteria (a 2011 CALL table-of-contents page exists at [ritsumei.ac.jp](https://www.ritsumei.ac.jp/~nozawa/callj/2011.html) but the article page itself was not reached). Treat the citation as unverified here.

## Key Question 7 — Feedback: immediate vs delayed; showing the correct answer after an error

### Takeaway
Showing the correct answer after an *error* is one of the largest effects in the whole literature (Pashler et al. 2005: 1-week retention up 494% for items answered wrong); feedback after a *correct* answer adds almost nothing, even for low-confidence correct answers. Immediate vs delayed feedback makes little difference for adults as long as the learner attends to it (Metcalfe 2017); errors made during effortful retrieval are not harmful when corrected.

### Cited Findings
- **[L2 vocab] Pashler, Cepeda, Wixted & Rohrer (2005)**, "When does feedback facilitate learning of words?", *JEP: Learning, Memory & Cognition*, 31(1), 3–8. 258 subjects learned Luganda–English pairs; after 2 exposures they were tested several times with feedback type varied between subjects; final test 1 week later. Supplying the correct answer after an incorrect response improved initial-session performance and **increased final retention by 494%**; feedback after correct responses made little difference immediately or at a delay, regardless of confidence. — [Author PDF (York U)](https://www.yorku.ca/ncepeda/publications/PCWR2005.pdf); [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1773)
- **[review] Metcalfe (2017)**, "Learning from errors", *Annual Review of Psychology*, 68, 465–489. Avoiding errors is unnecessary and can be counterproductive (makes practice easy and predictable); errors followed by corrective feedback are informative and can create a distinctive episodic memory. On timing: college students performed equally well with immediate vs delayed feedback, whereas children in grades 3–5 did better with delayed feedback; the key moderator across studies is whether learners *attend* to the feedback. — [Annual Reviews vol 68 listing](https://annualreviewsnews.org/2017/01/18/the-annual-review-of-psychology-volume-68/); [Secondary summary (Learning Scientists)](https://learningscientists.org/blog/tag/feedback); [Firth summary](https://firth.substack.com/p/how-we-learn-from-errors)
- **[L2 vocab meta-analysis] Kim & Webb (2022)**: feedback timing was one of the moderators explaining variance in spacing effect sizes. — [SSLLT summary](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/49487)
- **[general PA] Karpicke & Roediger (2007)**: the equal-spacing advantage held "both with and without feedback after test trials". — [WUSTL](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expanding-retrieval-practice-promotes-short-term-retention-but-eq/)

### Inferences
- App rule: after a wrong answer, always show the correct answer and make the learner look at it (e.g., require a keypress/short re-type) — this is the highest-leverage feedback behaviour. After a correct answer, feedback can be minimal (a tick) to save time.
- Immediate feedback is fine for adult self-study; there is no evidence that deliberately delaying the reveal helps adults, and it adds friction.
- Pashler 2005 also implies that a failed retrieval followed by the answer is *not* a wasted trial — so the scheduler can safely let difficulty be high enough that errors occur (see Q8).

### Gaps
- No study found on *how* the answer should be shown after an error for L2 vocabulary (re-type vs read only); the re-type suggestion above is an inference.

## Key Question 8 — Desirable difficulties and the optimal retrieval-success rate (85% rule?)

### Takeaway
"Desirable difficulty" is well supported for vocabulary (delayed first retrieval, productive direction, spacing all reduce in-session performance but improve retention). The "85% rule" (Wilson et al. 2019) was derived for *binary classification / perceptual learning with gradient-descent-like learners*, not for declarative recall, so it should not be cited as evidence for a 85% target in flashcards. The flashcard-specific numbers come instead from software optimisation: FSRS/Anki default 90% desired retention, with workload-minimising retention commonly estimated at ~80–85%, and from classroom retrieval-practice guidance of ~80% success.

### Cited Findings
- **[not L2; theoretical/ML + perceptual learning] Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019)**, "The Eighty Five Percent Rule for optimal learning", *Nature Communications*, 10, 4646, DOI 10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4. For a broad class of learning algorithms on **binary classification tasks**, the optimal training error rate is ~15.87% (accuracy ~85%); demonstrated on artificial and biologically plausible neural networks and consistent with prior animal perceptual-learning studies. The authors say it applies most to perceptual learning (e.g., a radiologist distinguishing tumours). — [PMC6831579](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579); [Brown news](https://www.brown.edu/news/2019-11-07); [Arizona news](https://executive.eller.arizona.edu/news/2019/12/if-you-want-improve-learning-try-85-rule)
- **[L2 vocab] Nakata dissertation (2013)**: "difficult, effortful retrieval practice enhanced long-term retention more effectively than easy learning conditions"; massed learning looked best during practice but was worst on delayed tests. — [LINGUIST List](https://linguistlist.org/issues/24/3797)
- **[general PA] Karpicke & Roediger (2007)**: delaying the first retrieval (making it harder) is the active ingredient for long-term retention. — [WUSTL](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/expanding-retrieval-practice-promotes-short-term-retention-but-eq/)
- **[L2 vocab] Schneider, Healy & Bourne (2002)**: harder training (L1→L2, contextual interference) → more forgetting-resistant memory at 1 week. — [CU Boulder](https://experts.colorado.edu/display/pubid_52638)
- **[general PA] Kornell (2009)**: 72% of learners preferred the less effective (easier-feeling) small-stack method even after experiencing both. **[L2 vocab] Karpicke & Roediger (2008)**: learners' predictions were uncorrelated with actual retention. Together these show that subjective ease is a bad guide to scheduling. — [Learning & the Brain summary](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/flashcard-strategies/); [Purdue](https://www.purdue.edu/uns/x/2008a/080214KarpickeTest.html)
- **[practice guidance]** Classroom retrieval-practice guidance recommends pitching retrieval so learners succeed ~80% of the time ("effortful and successful"). — [Research School (Bradford)](https://researchschool.org.uk/bradford/news/retrieval-practice-how-hard)
- **[software, non-peer-reviewed]** FSRS: desired retention default 0.9; higher values increase review workload roughly exponentially; "compute minimum recommended retention" typically lands at 0.80–0.85 for long-term knowledge, with 0.90 described by some users as over-practice. — [Anki forums CMRR](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/about-minimum-recommended-retention-cmrr/54501/2); [FSRS tuning guide](https://jiten.moe/guides/tuning-fsrs)

### Inferences
- A target *predicted* recall probability at review time of ~0.80–0.90 is defensible for the app: it is the range used by the largest real-world SRS deployments, it is consistent with "effortful but mostly successful" retrieval guidance, and it sits well above the point where errors dominate (Pashler 2005 shows errors are recoverable if corrected). Going below ~0.8 trades knowledge for workload; going above ~0.9 inflates workload fast.
- Do not cite Wilson et al. (2019) as the justification; cite the desirable-difficulty studies plus spacing-model workload curves. If the app exposes a slider, 0.85 is a reasonable default with 0.80–0.95 bounds.
- Because learners' felt ease is uncorrelated with real retention, the app should *not* let users shorten intervals based on confidence after a correct answer; confidence ratings are useful mainly for grading partial errors.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed L2-vocabulary study was found that varies target retrieval-success probability parametrically and measures long-term retention per unit time; the 0.8–0.9 range rests on software telemetry and theory, not RCTs.
- The Research School "~80%" figure is practitioner guidance, not a primary empirical result.
